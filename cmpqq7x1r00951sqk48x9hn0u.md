I don't believe that anybody gets into cybersecurity because they're passionate about data classification (and if anyone reads this by any chance, that is, my bad) I’ll say that plainly. But the more I learnt about Purview, I’ve learnt about how organisations actually deal and organize data, the more I've come to respect what Microsoft Purview is trying to solve (from what i understand).

Purview is Microsoft's information protection and compliance platform. In practical terms, it's the toolset that helps organisations answer questions like: where does our sensitive data live, who has access to it, and what happens if someone tries to move it somewhere they shouldn't? The Applied Skills credential I completed focuses specifically on information protection and data loss prevention, DLP for short.

DLP is exactly what it sounds like. Policies that detect and prevent sensitive information, such as financial records and personal identity numbers,  prevent confidential contracts from leaving the organisation in ways that aren't authorised. An email with a customer's ID number is going to a personal Gmail account. A file with payment card details is being uploaded to an unauthorized cloud service. Purview sits in the background watching and, in most cases, waiting for those moments.

What I found interesting working through this wasn't the technology itself; it was the thought process behind it. Building a DLP policy isn't just a technical exercise; you have to understand what data matters to the business, why it matters, and what the realistic risk scenarios are. That part of the business context and the technical implementation of it is where my background in organisational behaviour actually started to feel relevant. I know that it's not the flashiest corner of cybersecurity at all, and there are no war stories about catching hackers with Purview (as far as I know). But data leaks quietly, often from the inside, and often without anyone intending harm (most times). The tools that prevent that tend to operate in the background as the unsung heroes, largely invisible when they're working.
